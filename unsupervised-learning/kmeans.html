K-means

K-Means Clustering

Grouping US weather stations into climate zones using winter temperature and yearly precipitation.

Scenario

Climate zones are one of the most widely used maps in geography. The USDA plant hardiness zones on seed packets, the climate zones in building energy codes, and the Köppen map in most textbooks all group places that have similar climates. Grouping is useful because places with similar climates deal with similar issues: how much heating homes need, which crops grow well, and how much water a city has to store for the dry months.

Most of these maps are built from rules that people decided on in advance. Köppen, for example, calls a place a desert if its yearly precipitation is below a cutoff that depends on its temperature. In this activity you'll work the other way around. You'll start with the data and let the patterns in it decide the groups.

You have climate data for 70 weather stations across the lower 48 states. K-means will sort them into groups so that stations with similar climates end up together. You choose how many groups you want (that number is K). You don't tell it what the groups should be or give it examples of correct answers, which is why K-means is called unsupervised learning. At the end you'll name your groups and compare them with the Köppen zones, to see where a data-driven map agrees with the traditional one and where it doesn't.

The data

Each weather station has two numbers. Both are 30-year averages (1991–2020).

January temperature

degrees Fahrenheit (°F)

The average temperature in January, combining daytime highs and nighttime lows. It tells you how cold winters get.

Why we use it: summers are warm across most of the US, but winters are very different from place to place, from 8°F in Fargo, ND to 70°F in Key West, FL. So winter temperature does a better job of telling climates apart than summer temperature.

Yearly precipitation

inches per year

The total amount of rain plus melted snow that falls in an average year.

Why we use it: it separates dry places like Las Vegas (4 inches) from wet places like Mobile, AL (65 inches). Temperature and precipitation together are the starting point for most climate classifications.

Location is not part of the data. K-means only sees the two climate numbers. It never sees latitude or longitude. We use the coordinates afterward, only to put the results on a map. So if the groups form clear regions on the map, that pattern came from the climate data alone.
Both variables are rescaled. Before running K-means, each variable is rescaled to run from 0 (the lowest station) to 1 (the highest station). This keeps one variable from counting more than the other just because its numbers are bigger.

Part 1

Run K-means one step at a time

Each dot is one weather station, placed by its January temperature (left to right) and its yearly precipitation (bottom to top). Stations that are close together on this plot have similar climates. Hover over a dot to see which station it is.

K-means divides the dots into K groups so that every station is as close as possible to the center of its group. Follow the steps on the right to see how it does that.

How K-means works
Choose KHow many groups do you want?
  1. 1Place starting centers

    Click on the plot to place 3 centers. These are just starting guesses, so anywhere works.

  2. 2Assign stations to the nearest center

    Each station joins the group of whichever center is closest to it on the plot.

  3. 3Move each center to the middle of its group

    Each center moves to the average January temperature and average precipitation of the stations in its group.

  4. 4Repeat steps 2 and 3

    After the centers move, some stations may now be closer to a different center, so they're assigned again. When no station changes groups, K-means is finished.

Within-group spread: –
This adds up how far each station is from its group's center (each distance is squared first). K-means is trying to make this number as small as possible, and it should go down with each step.

Choosing K

K-means doesn't decide how many groups there should be. You do. One way to decide is to run K-means for several values of K and check the within-group spread each time. That's the same number you watched go down in Part 1.

Adding groups always lowers the spread, because every station ends up closer to some center. So the goal isn't the lowest number. Look for the point where adding another group stops helping much. On the plot, that shows up as a bend in the line, often called the elbow.

The elbow is a guide, not a rule. A good K also has to make sense for your purpose. Can you describe each group in a few words? Would the groups be useful?

Questions to discuss
  1. Which of your groups line up well with a single Köppen zone? Which ones mix several zones together?
  2. Find Seattle and Portland, OR. Which K-means group are they in, and which stations is that group mostly made of? Köppen puts them in the same zone as San Francisco. Why might the two approaches disagree? (Hint: what does Köppen look at that our two variables don't?)
  3. Climate changes gradually across space, but every station has to belong to exactly one group. Find a station near the edge of its group on the map, such as Kansas City or Oklahoma City. Try a few values of K. Does it always stay in the same group?
  4. If you could add a third variable, what would you add? How do you think it would change the groups?
Limitations of K-means

It only knows what you give it. Our data has winter temperature and total precipitation. It has nothing about summer heat or which part of the year the precipitation falls in, so K-means can't tell Seattle's dry summers apart from Philadelphia's wet ones.

Every station has to belong to exactly one group. Real climates blend into each other gradually, but K-means draws hard lines. A station that sits between two groups goes to whichever center is slightly closer.

It works best when groups are round and similar in size. K-means measures straight-line distance to a center, so it tends to make compact groups of similar size. If the real groups are long and stretched out, or very different in size, it can split or merge them in ways that don't make sense.

The starting centers can change the result. As you may have seen in Part 1, different starting centers can lead to different final groups. In practice, people run K-means many times and keep the run with the lowest within-group spread, which is what Part 2 does.

You have to choose K and interpret the groups. The algorithm doesn't know how many climate zones there "should" be or what they mean. Those decisions come from you and your knowledge of geography.
